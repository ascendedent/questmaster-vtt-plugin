# Improv options

## The core principle

The world responds to what the players did; it does not reset. Every option is a natural consequence of the action, not a detour back to the plan. If an option only makes sense because the players' choice did not matter, cut it.

## Finding the core tension

Ask three things, fast:

1. **What changed in the world?** Not "they deviated", but the fact: the quest-giver is dead, the bridge is down, the guard has switched sides.
2. **Who noticed?** Witnesses, people with a stake, people who were counting on the old state of things.
3. **What is now unanswered?** That question is the core tension. "Who inherits the dead man's debts, and do they know the party did it?"

## Consequence engines

Pull options from different engines so they genuinely differ.

- **Someone with a stake acts.** A faction, a family, a creditor, a rival moves because of what happened.
- **The cost comes due.** What the players spent or broke shows up: noise, a lost ally, a burned bridge, a broken promise.
- **The door they opened.** The action reveals a place, a person or a fact that was always there.
- **The clock lurches.** The antagonist's plan speeds up, slows down, or changes shape in response.
- **The bystander.** Someone small and specific was affected and now wants something.
- **The misread.** The world interprets the action wrongly: the party looks like heroes, villains, or someone's agents.

## Tone palette

Give each option a different tone.

- **Escalation:** stakes rise now. Use when the table has energy to spend.
- **Opportunity:** the deviation pays off in an unexpected way. Use to reward bold play.
- **Twist:** the action reveals the situation was not what it seemed. Use sparingly, and only when it fits canon.
- **Emotional:** someone the party cares about feels it. Use to slow the table down.
- **Practical:** the world deals with it matter-of-factly; logistics become the scene. Use when the night needs grounding.
- **Lateral:** an angle nobody at the table expected, still caused by the action. Often option C.

## Agency checks

For each option:

- The players still have a real choice in the next five minutes.
- The option does not require them to undo what they did.
- Its cost is fair: it follows from the fiction, not from the DM's frustration.
- It leaves room for the plan's best material to return in a new form (the dungeon's treasure can be moved, the villain's speech can happen elsewhere).

## Bridges: buying a minute right now

One sentence, used while the DM reads the options.

- A dog starts barking at one of the characters and won't stop.
- The lamp gutters out; someone has to find a light.
- A child tugs a character's sleeve: "Are you the ones who did it?"
- A bell rings somewhere close, three times, then stops.
- The NPC nearest the party drops something fragile, and it breaks.
- Rain starts, hard enough that everyone in the street moves for cover.
- A messenger runs past shouting a name the party knows.
- An NPC asks the bluntest question in the room: "So what happens now?"
- The smell of smoke, from a direction nobody expected.
- A stranger sits down at the party's table uninvited and orders for them.

A good bridge commits to nothing. It can lead into any of the options.

## Worked example (original)

**The situation:** asked to escort the toll-keeper to the guild for trial, the party instead put her on a barge downriver with the evidence.
**What was planned:** a courtroom scene at the guild hall with the factor as prosecutor.
**Core tension:** the guild expected a prisoner and a ledger, and has neither. Who does it blame, and what does it do tonight?

**RIGHT NOW:** the guild hall's door opens and the factor's clerk steps out, looks at the empty space between the party, and says, "Where is she?"

**A. The Empty Dock** (escalation)
What happens: the factor declares the party accomplices and posts a reward for the toll-keeper. Guild bargemen start searching downriver, and the party's own boat is impounded at the ferry.
New dynamic: a pursuit downriver, with the party behind and the guild ahead.
Players can: race to reach her first, go to ground, or negotiate their boat back.
Upside: turns a planned talky scene into a chase with a clear goal. Cost: the guild becomes an open enemy tonight.

**B. The Grateful Ward** (opportunity)
What happens: word spreads that someone stood up to the guild. The levee crew's foreman finds the party and offers shelter, labor and a cart; the lower ward closes ranks around them.
New dynamic: the party gains a community that will hide them, and owes it something.
Players can: accept and become the ward's champions, or keep their distance and stay free.
Upside: deepens the town as a place the party belongs. Cost: the ward will expect them to stay and fight.

**C. The Factor's Relief** (lateral)
What happens: the factor is privately pleased. Without a trial, the ledger never becomes public, and her own name, which is in it, stays hidden. She sends the party a quiet thank-you and a job offer.
New dynamic: the antagonist is now trying to recruit them, and the ledger has become dangerous to its holder.
Players can: take the job and work her from inside, expose her, or warn the toll-keeper.
Upside: reframes the villain without contradicting anything. Cost: only works if the factor's name in the ledger fits canon; check before using.

**THREADS TOUCHED:** the midsummer charter: accelerated. The drowned courier: reframed (the ledger is now the prize).
**NEW THREAD?:** "The barge downriver": where the toll-keeper is, and who reaches her first.

## Keeping it if the DM asks

If the DM says "keep the foreman" or "make that a thread", search for the name first, then draft `upsert_npc` or `upsert_thread`, recap with `get_changeset`, and ask before `request_approval`. Which option was used, and how it played out, belongs in the session's recap.
