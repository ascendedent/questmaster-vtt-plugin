# In-world document registers

One event, five sources, five registers. Register and source travel together: a terse register suits a ledger or a marker, a lush one suits a commissioned chronicle. Each source bends the event in its own direction, and one truth layer sits underneath all five.

## The event

The weir at Dunnet's Reach broke in the spring flood and drowned the lower town of Mossford.

## Terse OSR: the toll ledger

Source: the Reach toll-keeper's ledger, written the morning after, for the Millers' Compact that employs him.

> Third of Thaw. Weir gone at the night bell. Lower town under. Dead counted 41; more not counted. Toll suspended. Mason Orwin sought for questioning. Reward 10 silver.

- **Fits:** sandbox and old-school tables; a find that rewards close reading.
- **The bias:** pure omission. It names one man and nothing else, never says why he is sought, and records no repair dates at all.
- **Use it as:** a ledger page the party can copy in five seconds.

## Lush literary: the commissioned elegy

Source: "A Lament for Lower Mossford", written a year later by a poet the Millers' Compact paid, read aloud at the memorial.

> The river came for Mossford in the dark of Thaw, when the old stones of the weir, long trusted and lately tended by an unworthy hand, gave way. Forty and more went down into the brown water. Yet the Compact did not abandon its own: it opened its granaries, it raised this stone, and it carved upon it every name the river took.

- **Fits:** gothic, mythic or melancholy campaigns; a monument or a ceremony scene.
- **The bias:** blame in a single phrase ("an unworthy hand"), then a long look at the patron's generosity. Beautiful, sincere in tone, and paid for.
- **Use it as:** a plaque on the memorial or a speech at the anniversary.

## Pulp: the broadsheet

Source: a printer's broadsheet sold in the market square three days after the flood.

> MASON'S BOTCH DROWNS MOSSFORD! Forty souls swept away when villain HALE ORWIN'S shoddy patchwork bursts at midnight! The coward fled by moonlight with his tools and his wages! Ten silver to the stout fellow who drags him back to face the Compact!

- **Fits:** swashbuckling and action campaigns; handouts players love to read aloud in a voice.
- **The bias:** loud certainty. The printer has no evidence, only the Compact's version and a story that sells.
- **Use it as:** a sheet nailed to every post, or one blowing across the road.

## Noir: the clerk's letter

Source: a private letter from Pim Arless, junior clerk of the Millers' Compact, to his brother. Never sent; found folded in the clerk's boot.

> They had me copy the repair book clean last week. New pages, old dates, Orwin's name on every repair that was never paid for. I did it. I'm good at copying. What bothers me is that I read the old pages first, and Orwin wrote three times that the east footing was going. Three times. Burn this, or don't. I haven't decided which of us I'm protecting.

- **Fits:** intrigue and investigation campaigns; the moment the party finds the crack in the official story.
- **The bias:** self-serving guilt. Pim tells the truth about the book and nothing about himself, and he does not know where the money went.
- **Use it as:** the key clue, staged to whoever searches the clerk.

## Storybook: the skipping rhyme

Source: a rhyme Mossford's children chant over the rope a few years later; nobody remembers who made it up.

> Mason, mason, mend the wall,
> Mend it once and mend it all.
> Mason wrote it, nobody read,
> Down came the water over our heads.

- **Fits:** fairy-tale and family campaigns, or any campaign where folk memory knows more than the officials do.
- **The bias:** folk compression. It blames the mason in the first line and quietly acquits him in the third, because children repeat what grown-ups whispered.
- **Use it as:** a sound the party hears in the street, free, on their first visit.

## The truth layer under all five (DM only)

- **True:** the Millers' Compact cut the weir's upkeep to pay a new river toll. Hale Orwin, the stonemason, warned them in writing three times that the east footing was failing. After the flood the Compact had the repair book rewritten to blame him, and he fled because he expected to hang.
- **Distorted:** the ledger implies guilt by omission, the elegy by one phrase, the broadsheet by assertion. The clerk's letter is accurate but partial. The rhyme is closest to the truth and the least believed.
- **Why:** the Compact owes Mossford's survivors compensation if the failure was its own neglect; a guilty mason makes the debt disappear.
- **Points to:** the original repair book (Pim kept the old pages), Orwin himself (doing day labor upriver under his mother's name), and the toll receipts that show where the upkeep money went.

## Reading the five together

- Hand the party two sources that disagree before you hand them the one that explains why. Contradiction is the hook; explanation is the reward.
- The most official source is the most polished and the least honest. The least official (a rhyme, a private letter) often holds the truth, mangled.
- Each source should sound like it could not have been written by any of the others. If two read alike, change the author, not the adjectives.

## Choosing a source by register

| Campaign register | Sources that sound right in it |
|---|---|
| Terse OSR | ledgers, waystones, inventories, tally sticks, bounty slips |
| Lush literary | chronicles, elegies, sermons, epitaphs, illuminated histories |
| Pulp | broadsheets, wanted posters, boasts, tavern tales, playbills |
| Noir | private letters, confessions, informers' reports, coded notes |
| Storybook | rhymes, fables, lullabies, sayings, painted signs |

Mix sources across registers inside one campaign on purpose: the official voice and the street voice should not agree.
