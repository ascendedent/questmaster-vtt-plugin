# Wilderness Hexcrawl: beats

The signature structure is a loop, not a line: leave a safe place, spend resources crossing country, find something, decide whether to push on or turn back, and carry the news home. Plot lives in what the party chooses to chase.

## Arc structure: the expedition (3 to 5 sessions)

1. **The charter.** Someone pays, begs or dares the party to go: map a basin, find a missing caravan, reach a peak before a rival. Name the deadline, the reward, and one thing the patron is not saying.
2. **The near country.** Known tracks, the last settlement, rumors that disagree. Low danger, high information. End on a fork the party picks.
3. **The deep country.** Supply starts to bite. Landmarks from the rumors turn out to be real, wrong or occupied. One discovery reframes the charter (the missing caravan was not lost; it deserted).
4. **The find.** A site worth the trip: a ruin, a lair, a valley of hot springs, the river's source. Usually a dungeon or a social set piece.
5. **The way home.** Shorter on paper, harder in practice: the weather turned, a companion is hurt, something is following. Never skip it.
6. **The consequence.** Back at base, the map is worth something to somebody. Who buys it, who steals it, who now marches up the route the party opened?

## Arc structure: the frontier season (a campaign spine)

- One base (fort, trading post, hill monastery) and 4 to 8 regions radiating from it, each with one headline site and one rumor that is wrong.
- Seasons as the macro clock: spring floods close the low fords, summer opens the high passes, autumn brings the herds and the raiders, winter shuts everything except the frozen river road.
- On every return to base, advance two things: one faction move, and one change the party caused.
- The season ends when the passes close. Whatever the party did not reach stays unknown until spring, and a rival may get there first.

## Session structure: one trip out

- **Opening (10 minutes):** read the supply count, set the day's weather, describe what the party can see from where it stands. Ask where they go.
- **The road (most of the session):** 2 to 4 travel days, each with one meaningful beat. Fast-forward empty stretches in one sentence, but still tick supply and weather.
- **The landmark beat:** at least one arrival at something they saw from far away.
- **The camp:** one night scene per session where characters talk, the watch roll happens, and something leaves a sign (tracks around the latrine, a dropped bead, drums to the north).
- **The closing decision:** end on a choice, not a fight. Push on with two days of food, or turn back with the map half drawn?

## Scene structure: the travel watch

Run each day as up to four watches (morning, afternoon, evening, night). For each watch that matters:

1. **Announce:** weather, visibility, what sits on the horizon.
2. **Choose:** route, pace (careful, normal, forced), and roles (navigate, scout, forage, guard).
3. **Roll once:** one navigation or encounter check, not five. Read the result as a sign, a sighting or an event.
4. **Spend:** tick rations, a torch, a strained mount, a day off the deadline.
5. **Show:** one sensory detail that makes this hex different from the last one.

## Scene structure: the vista

When the party crests a ridge, reaches a tree line or climbs a tower, pay it off with a vista: three things at three distances (near, middle, horizon), and at least one they have never heard of. A vista replaces a page of exposition and gives the players three destinations to argue about.

## Scene structure: the return

At base, run a short round where each player says what their character does with the news (sell it, hide it, tell the temple, get drunk and brag). Each answer moves a faction or a thread. This is where the sandbox becomes a story.

## Worked example: the Sallowmere Survey

**Charter.** Ottile Brask, a mapmaker in debt to a river guild, must deliver a survey of the Sallowmere basin within 18 days or lose her shop. She pays per landmark recorded and double for a usable pass. She does not know the guild has already sold the basin to a timber concern and only needs her survey to look official.

**Regions (one area each).**
- *Reedhollow Flats:* tall reeds, slow channels, biting flies at dusk. Landmark: a leaning lighthouse fifty miles from any sea.
- *The Knuckles:* five bare granite hills. Landmark: a ring of stones on the highest, smoke rising from it on still mornings.
- *Gallow Pines:* old forest, game trails too straight to be game trails. Landmark: an elk skull nailed head-high to a trunk, then another, then another.

**Session 1.** Leave Brask's Landing; choose the ferry (fast, the ferryman trades in news) or the reed causeway (free, rotten in places). Night one: lights moving in the reeds that stop when watched. Vista from the lighthouse: the Knuckles, the smoke, and a tent camp nobody mentioned.

**Session 2.** The tent camp is a rival survey working for the timber concern; they are friendly, well fed and two days ahead. The smoke on the Knuckles is a herder clan's signal fire. Their elder claims the basin by older right and wants an outside witness to walk the boundary stones. Supply check: day 7 of 18, rations for 9.

**Session 3.** In Gallow Pines the skulls mark the herders' boundary; past them, the trail ends at a charcoal burners' village sealed from inside (a dungeon site). Closing choice: carry home the true map, which ruins Brask and protects the herders, or the map the guild wants.

**Why it works.** Every session ends on a choice, every region has a landmark visible from the last one, and the charter's secret turns the map itself into treasure that factions will fight over.

## Prep checklist per region

- One landmark seen from the neighboring region, one hidden site, one wrong rumor.
- A weather table of 6 entries with one that changes the route, not just the mood.
- An encounter table of 6 to 8 situations, at least half of them not fights.
- One local who knows a shortcut and wants something for it.
- One thing that changes if the party never comes here.
