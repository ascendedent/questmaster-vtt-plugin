# War Campaign: Pitfalls

The ways a war campaign goes wrong, how to spot each one, and the fix.

## The party becomes the army
- **Spot:** the party kills hundreds, wins battles alone, and the generals wait for them before doing anything.
- **Fix:** the party is a lever. Their missions are the small, precise things that change what armies can do: the bridge, the courier, the engineer, the truce. Armies still do the fighting.

## The war as wallpaper
- **Spot:** the war is mentioned in every session but nothing ever moves; it is a backdrop for unrelated adventures.
- **Fix:** fronts with clocks, ticked in the war turn after every session and shown on-screen through a person or an object.

## The war that ignores the party
- **Spot:** fronts move on schedule no matter what the party does; the players feel like tourists.
- **Fix:** every mission has a written lever, and the ripple scene shows that lever working (or failing) before the session ends.

## Numbers without faces
- **Spot:** "Three thousand die at the ford." The table shrugs.
- **Fix:** one named person, one place, one object. The ferry-keeper's daughter, the ferry rope, the empty landing.

## Misery fatigue
- **Spot:** every session ends in loss; players stop investing because nothing they love survives.
- **Fix:** let the party save something outright at least once per arc (a village, a friend, a regiment's colors). Hope is what makes the losses hurt.

## The faceless enemy
- **Spot:** the enemy is an undifferentiated mass of targets with no reasons.
- **Fix:** an enemy officer with a grievance, a soldier met before the fight, a front where the enemy is right. Even an inhuman host needs a logic the party can learn and use.

## Mass battle as a slog
- **Spot:** a full battle resolved unit by unit for three hours of real time.
- **Fix:** the party plays their slice with full detail; the wider battle is a battlefield events list and a few front ticks. The result of the slice decides how the battle goes.

## Real war in costume
- **Spot:** the war recreates a specific real-world conflict, people or atrocity with the names swapped.
- **Fix:** build the war from invented causes, cultures and grudges. Borrow logistics and texture, never real peoples or real tragedies. See safety.md.

## Too many fronts
- **Spot:** seven fronts and the players cannot remember which matters.
- **Fix:** 2 to 4 fronts, at least one non-military. Merge or resolve one before adding another.

## War council paralysis
- **Spot:** an hour of strategy debate with no action.
- **Fix:** give the council a clock (the enemy moves at dawn), give each commander one hard fact, and end the scene when the party commits.

## The promotion trap
- **Spot:** the characters are promoted to command and spend sessions in a tent moving pins.
- **Fix:** command gives them better levers, not desks. A captain chooses which mission her company takes and still leads it into the field.

## Jargon overload
- **Spot:** flanks, enfilades, sappers and lines of communication; the players are lost.
- **Fix:** describe what people are doing in plain words ("they are digging under the wall"). Use one term of art per scene, and have an NPC explain it in character.

## Consequences that never arrive
- **Spot:** the party burned a village's grain to deny the enemy, and nothing came of it.
- **Fix:** keep a consequences ledger in the war turn. Every cruel shortcut and every mercy comes back, ideally in the aftermath arc.

## The predetermined decisive battle
- **Spot:** the final battle's outcome is fixed no matter what the party did.
- **Fix:** the odds of the turn depend on which clocks the party held back. Write at least three outcomes (clean victory, costly victory, defeat with a way forward) and let play decide.

## The enemy as cartoon villain
- **Spot:** the invaders kick dogs and laugh.
- **Fix:** cruelty in war comes from orders, fear, hunger and grief. Show the reason, even when the act is unforgivable.
