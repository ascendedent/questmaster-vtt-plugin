# Fey Fairytale: beats

The signature structure is the bargain: a want, an offer, a price, a benefit enjoyed, and a reckoning when the terms come due. Arcs are built from bargains made, kept, bent and broken, and from crossings into a place where the rules are different.

## Anatomy of a bargain

Write all six parts before the bargain appears at the table:

1. **The want:** what the mortal needs badly enough to deal (rain, a cure, a child home, a door opened).
2. **The wording:** the exact sentence spoken. Read it aloud at the table and hand it over as text.
3. **The price:** personal and specific (a memory, a sense, a season of service, a name, the color of your eyes).
4. **The term:** when it falls due, or what condition ends it.
5. **The loophole:** at least one in the mortal's favor and one in the fey's, both findable by a careful reading.
6. **The breach:** what happens if either side breaks it. Fey breaches are rare and catastrophic for the fey; mortal breaches are common and costly.

## Arc structure: the debt (3 to 5 sessions)

1. **The benefit.** The party meets the bargain from the comfortable side: a village that never floods, a knight who never loses, a family that is always lucky.
2. **The crack.** Something is wrong: the price is being paid by someone who never agreed to it, or the term is close, or the benefit is failing.
3. **The terms.** The party finds the wording (a carving, a rhyme, a witness) and reads it with fresh eyes.
4. **The collector.** The fey sends someone to collect or renegotiate. Courtesy matters here more than steel.
5. **The reckoning.** The party pays, finds the loophole, offers a new bargain, or breaks the deal and faces the breach. Each choice should be possible.

## Arc structure: the stolen (3 to 4 sessions)

1. **The taking.** Someone is gone: a sibling, a bride, a bard, a hound. A sign of the fey is left (a ring of mushrooms, a shoe full of petals).
2. **The crossing.** The way in requires a rule kept (walk backward round the stone, go at the turn of the tide, carry nothing iron).
3. **Three tests.** Hospitality, a riddle or game, and a choice between something lovely and something true.
4. **The trade.** The stolen one is released only for a price, a substitute, or a winning wager.
5. **The return.** Time was different there. A day under the hill is a season above, or a year is an hour. Show what changed.

## Arc structure: the curse and its key

- Every curse has an origin (a broken promise, a stolen gift, an insult at a christening), a sign (a body changed, a house that cannot be left, a voice that cannot speak), and a key (a deed, an object, a word, a sacrifice).
- Hide the key in three parts across the arc. Each part costs something to gather.
- Offer two keys: the one the curse names, and a second one the players can bargain for, with a worse price.

## Session structure: a night in the otherworld

- **The threshold:** the crossing and its rule. Make the rule known before it bites.
- **The welcome:** a host, a feast or a gate-keeper, and the house rules stated in-world.
- **The test:** courtesy, wit or nerve. One NPC is kind in a way that will cost the party later.
- **The offer:** someone wants something from the party and makes a bargain.
- **The way back:** leaving costs something or takes a rule, and time has done something strange.

## Scene structure: the offer

1. **The gift first.** The fey gives something freely: food, a song, a flower. Accepting creates a small obligation.
2. **The terms.** Spoken plainly and exactly. The fey does not lie; it may leave things out.
3. **The pause.** Give the players time. Let them ask questions; answer each one truthfully and narrowly.
4. **The binding.** A handshake, a drop of blood, a name spoken three times, a ribbon tied. Make it a ritual so it is clear when the bargain is made.
5. **The seal.** One beautiful, wrong detail that tells the players the deal is real (the ribbon tightens by itself; the moon blinks).

## Worked example: the Thistlewick rain

**The bargain.** Forty years ago, the five elders of Thistlewick asked the Lady of the Hollow Oak for rain in a drought. Wording: "Rain shall fall on Thistlewick whenever we ask, for as long as the bell hangs in the tower." Price: each harvest, one memory from the eldest of every household.

**The crack.** The party arrives in a drought. The village elders are forgetting their own children's names. The bell tower is cracked and leaning.

**The loopholes.** For the village: "the bell" is not "this bell"; recast it and hang a new one, and the bargain continues on a fresh bell. For the Lady: "whenever we ask" meant all five elders asking together, and only two are still alive, so she owes no rain at all. The village has been paying for nothing for three years.

**Session 1.** Arrive; meet the elders; find the wording carved inside the bell. Vista of the Hollow Oak across the river, too green for a drought.
**Session 2.** Cross by the stepping stones (rule: never step on the seventh, it belongs to her). Feast at the oak; the Lady's steward, Master Pell Gloaming, offers terms: the village may end the bargain if it returns every memory's worth in kind.
**Session 3.** The reckoning. Options the DM can present: argue the "we" loophole and demand the memories back as overpayment; recast the bell and renegotiate a fairer price; break the deal by taking the bell down and face the breach (the river turns to meadow and the oak's servants come to collect); or offer a new bargain that someone in the party pays.

**Why it works.** The players read the contract, both sides have a loophole, and every ending costs something different.

## Clocks for bargains

- Track each bargain as a thread whose urgency rises with each session toward its term.
- A broken rule advances its bargain one step immediately; a kept courtesy can buy a step back.
