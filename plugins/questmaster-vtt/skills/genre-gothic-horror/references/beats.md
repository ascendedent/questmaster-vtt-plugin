# Gothic Horror: Beats

The signature structure is the dread ladder. It works at every scale: an arc climbs it over sessions, a session climbs it from dusk to dawn, a scene climbs it in ten minutes. Know which rung you are on and never skip one.

## The dread ladder

### Rung 1: Unease
- **Job:** tell the players this place is not neutral, without giving them anything they can name.
- **Tools:** hospitality slightly too generous (their rooms were aired before they wrote ahead); a house rule with no reason given ("the east stair is not used after the lamps are lit"); fresh candle wax in a wing everyone says is closed; silence where sound belongs (no birds in the orchard, no dogs in the yard).
- **Rule:** everything on this rung has a mundane explanation a player could accept. Nothing is provably supernatural yet.
- **Climb when:** a player asks a question about the house that every NPC deflects.

### Rung 2: Wrongness
- **Job:** one small thing that cannot be explained away.
- **Tools:** an object moved between visits to a locked room; a portrait whose painted eyes were closed yesterday; a servant who answers a question nobody asked aloud; a door that opens onto the room you just left; a child's handprint on the inside of a window three floors up.
- **Rule:** let the party test it. If they scatter flour on the floor or tie a thread across the door, something answers the test.
- **Climb when:** the party has evidence and a theory, even a wrong one.

### Rung 3: Revelation
- **Job:** the truth, or the worst part of it, arrives through an object or a person, and it implicates someone alive.
- **Tools:** the second will, the diary with pages cut out (and the stubs still legible), the crypt with one coffin too many, a confession dragged out of someone who loves the family.
- **Rule:** a revelation must change what the party wants to do. If it only confirms what they suspected, it is still wrongness.
- **Climb when:** the party knows what the curse is and roughly what breaking it would cost.

### Rung 4: Confrontation
- **Job:** the party faces the monster, the family or the choice, on ground the house picks.
- **Rule:** offer at least two endings that are not "kill it": bargain, break the curse, flee with someone, inherit it, take its place.
- **After:** the bill. Mercy, destruction and bargain each leave a cost that lands next session.

### Falling back down
- After a confrontation, drop to unease, never to normal. Something survived, someone paid, the house is quieter but not empty.
- A second climb should be faster than the first: the players know the rungs now, so use fewer unease beats and a sharper wrongness.

## Arc structure (3 to 5 sessions)

1. **The arrival.** A reason to come (a will, a betrothal, a commission, a body to bury) and a reason to stay that the players choose (money owed, a person to protect, curiosity). Unease only.
2. **The household.** Meet the family and staff, each with a want. One wrongness at night. End on a question.
3. **The crack.** Someone dies, vanishes or confesses. Wrongness piles up; the party starts investigating rather than visiting.
4. **The sin.** Revelation: the origin act, the living beneficiary, the price of breaking it. The party picks a side.
5. **The choice and the bill.** Confrontation, then the cost of whatever they chose, carried forward as a thread.

Short arc (2 sessions): merge 1 and 2, merge 3 and 4.

## Session structure: day and night

- **Daylight (business):** social scenes, legwork, the village, the library. The house is almost charming. Seed two objects and one lie.
- **Dusk (the turn):** a ritual of the house (lamps lit in a fixed order, doors locked, a prayer said at table). Someone breaks it, or the party is asked to keep it without explanation.
- **Night (the rung):** climb exactly one rung. Isolate the party into twos and threes if the table enjoys it; keep them together if it does not.
- **Dawn (the cost):** what the night took. A servant gone, a mirror cracked, a character who slept and remembers someone else's dream.

## Scene structure: four beats

1. **The normal:** describe the room as a room. Furniture, light, one comfortable detail.
2. **The detail:** one thing out of place, described flatly and without emphasis (the dining chairs are set for nine; there are eight of you).
3. **The test:** a player touches, asks or counts. Reward the action with more information, never with a jump scare.
4. **The answer:** the house replies in kind. Count the chairs again and there are eight, and the ninth place setting is in a player's hand.

## Worked example: The Third Daughter of Corvel House

**Premise.** The party escorts a notary to Corvel House, an orchard estate above the village of Cauldwell, to read old Lord Abelard Corvel's will. The family is gracious. The orchards are the only ones in the valley that never blight.

**The curse.** Sixty years ago Lady Imsa Corvel bargained with whatever lives under the oldest tree: the orchards would never fail, and the third daughter of each generation would go into the roots at sixteen. Imsa is still down there, half bark, half memory. The current third daughter, Wren, turns sixteen in eleven days.

**Living beneficiary.** Everyone. The family's wealth, the village's work, the church's roof. The village council chose silence long ago.

**Session 1 (unease).** The reading of the will. A codicil leaves Wren "to the orchard" and the family laughs it off as the old man's poetry. Apples on the table in midwinter. The east stair rule. Plant: Wren's sketchbook full of the same tree.

**Session 2 (wrongness).** Night: someone sings a lullaby in the orchard in Abelard's voice. A portrait of Imsa has fresh sap seeping from the canvas. The housekeeper, Mother Tamsel, straightens everything the party moves. Wren asks a player to promise to take her to the coast "before my birthday".

**Session 3 (revelation).** The church ledger lists every third daughter with the same word beside her name: rooted. Imsa's diary in the sealed nursery names the bargain. Wren's mother confesses she lost her own sister to the roots, swore it would never happen again, and then said nothing for sixteen years because the orchards paid for everything.

**Session 4 (confrontation).** Under the tree, Imsa offers terms. Options the DM can lay out:
- **Break the bargain:** Wren lives; the orchards blight next spring and Cauldwell faces a hungry year. The family turns on the party.
- **Take Wren and run:** the bargain claims the next third daughter in the village instead, a child the party met in session 1.
- **Renegotiate:** Imsa accepts a volunteer, years of someone's life, or a player character's memory of a loved one.
- **Free Imsa:** she wants to die; doing so ends the bargain and something older under the tree is now unbound.

**The bill.** Whatever they chose becomes an `upsert_thread` with urgency: the blight, the village child, the unbound thing, the family's vendetta.

## Mercy costs menu

When pricing mercy, pick one that hurts this party specifically:
- Time: the mercy takes the night, and something else happens unwatched.
- Standing: the village or the family will never trust the party again.
- Safety: the spared creature will feed again, rarely, and the party will hear of it.
- Self: a character keeps a mark, a debt, a dream that is not theirs.
- Others: someone innocent and off-screen carries the price, and the party learns their name.
