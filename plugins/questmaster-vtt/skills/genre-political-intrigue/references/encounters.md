# Political intrigue: encounters

An intrigue encounter is a situation where something the party wants is decided by people who want other things. Design each one with **stakes** (what changes when it ends), **complications** (what goes wrong on its own), and **outs** (how the party leaves if it turns against them). Keep numbers to QuestMaster: when steel comes out, `plan_encounter` and `rate_encounter` set the difficulty.

## Social encounters

### The council vote
- **Stakes:** A law, a charter or an appointment passes or fails. Each faction's agenda moves a step either way.
- **Shape:** Count votes before the session. Write each councillor's default vote, what would change it, and who already owns it.
- **Complications:** A councillor is absent (ill, detained, dead). A procedural motion delays the vote to tomorrow. A petition from outside the hall demands to be read.
- **Outs:** Abstention, delay by motion, a public challenge to someone's right to vote.

### The banquet
- **Stakes:** Who is seen with whom. Alliances are announced by seating, not speeches.
- **Shape:** Five to eight short conversations available; the party can attend three before the toasts. Each conversation offers one piece of leverage, one debt, or one warning.
- **Complications:** An uninvited guest with a legitimate claim to a seat; a toast that forces everyone to choose a side; a dish nobody touches because of a rumour.
- **Outs:** Leaving early (noticed and remembered), feigning illness, a staged accident.

### The audience
- **Stakes:** A ruler grants or refuses a request in a few minutes, in front of witnesses.
- **Shape:** One petition, one counter-petition from a rival, one question from the ruler that cannot be answered honestly without cost.
- **Complications:** The ruler is distracted by news delivered mid-audience; the rival has better standing; the herald mispronounces a name on purpose.
- **Outs:** Withdraw the petition (face lost, no debt incurred), accept a lesser grant with strings.

### The trial
- **Stakes:** Someone's life, title or land. Often a party ally, or the party itself.
- **Shape:** Charges, witnesses, the judge's own interest. List three facts the court will hear and one it must not.
- **Complications:** A witness recants; the judge is offered a bribe in view of the party; the law turns out to permit trial by combat.
- **Outs:** Appeal to a higher seat, a pardon bought with a debt, a confession by someone else.

## The three-sided negotiation

Use this procedure for any deal that matters.

1. Name the three sides and each one's **position**, **interest** and **walk-away point**.
2. Give each side one piece of **leverage** it will show and one it will hide.
3. Decide what the **third side** does if the first two get close to agreeing without it (it always does something).
4. List two deals that could plausibly close it, and what each costs the party in debt.
5. Present the DM those deals as options, not as the answer.

Example: the party brokers a ceasefire between a mercenary company and a border lord, while a temple holding both sides' ransom money mediates. The temple's interest is that the fighting continues long enough for the ransoms to mature.

## Combat as the failure state

### The assassination attempt
- **Stakes:** Who sent it matters more than whether the target survives. The fight is a clue.
- **Shape:** Two or three attackers, a crowded room, a target who may not be the obvious one. The assassins carry something that points at the wrong faction.
- **Complications:** Guards arrive and arrest whoever has a drawn blade, including the party. The target survives and blames the nearest rival.
- **Outs:** Protect the target and flee, pursue an attacker (into a chase scene), or let it happen and learn who benefits.

### The duel of honour
- **Stakes:** Reputation and a specific concession, set before the first pass.
- **Shape:** Rules agreed by seconds: first blood, yield, or death. The party may fight, second, or negotiate the terms.
- **Complications:** A second tampers with a blade; the opponent is a proxy for someone who cannot be seen fighting.
- **Outs:** Apology before the duel (a debt), a champion, an appeal to an older law.

### The riot
- **Stakes:** A district burns, or a faction proves it can control a crowd.
- **Shape:** Treat the crowd as terrain and a clock, not a monster. The party has a few rounds to reach a person, a door or a voice the crowd will hear.
- **Outs:** Calm the crowd, redirect it, or get out of the street.

## Exploration

- **The palace at night:** a map annotated with who is awake, where, and why. Every corridor encounter is a conversation before it is a fight. Use `annotate_map` for guard posts and private stairs, hidden until shown.
- **The archive:** records are leverage. The puzzle is not finding the document but knowing which one would hurt whom.
- **The country house:** a faction's retreat, where its members speak freely. Getting invited is the encounter.

## Puzzles

- **The seating chart:** a rival has to be seated at the banquet without insult. Each placement offends someone; the party chooses who.
- **The succession law:** two claims, one charter, an ambiguous clause. The party's reading decides who has standing.
- **The cipher letter:** decoding it is easy. Deciding whether to deliver it, copy it, or burn it is the puzzle.

## Escalation ladder

Climb one rung at a time, and let the party see each rung before the next.

1. **Whisper:** a rumour about the party, attributed to no one.
2. **Slight:** a public snub, a worse seat, an invitation withdrawn.
3. **Sanction:** a licence revoked, an ally told to keep distance, a debt called early.
4. **Seizure:** property, a person or a document taken under colour of law.
5. **Blood:** an attack, a duel forced, an arrest that becomes an execution.

Every rung offers an out: apologise, pay, trade leverage, or find a protector. Climbing past rung 3 should be a choice an NPC makes for reasons the party can learn.
